\section{Theory}

This section gives the theoretical background needed to interpret the turbulence models selected by \codeword{caseSetup}, the method used to choose ride-height runs for an aero map, and how the suspension kinematic solver moves the geometry. The equations below use the incompressible, constant-density form for clarity. The same modelling ideas apply to the compressible formulations, with the additional energy equation and density variations.

\subsection{Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS)}

In a turbulent flow, each instantaneous quantity is decomposed into a mean and a fluctuating component,
\begin{equation}
    u_i = \overline{u_i} + u_i', \qquad p = \overline{p} + p'.
\end{equation}
The averaging operation removes the rapidly varying fluctuations. Applying it to the incompressible continuity and momentum equations gives
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \overline{u_i}}{\partial x_i} = 0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial \overline{u_i}}{\partial t}
    + \overline{u_j}\frac{\partial \overline{u_i}}{\partial x_j}
    = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{\partial \overline{p}}{\partial x_i}
    + \nu\frac{\partial^2 \overline{u_i}}{\partial x_j\partial x_j}
    - \frac{\partial \overline{u_i' u_j'}}{\partial x_j}.
\end{equation}
The final term is the divergence of the Reynolds-stress tensor. It represents the transport of momentum by turbulent fluctuations and introduces more unknowns than equations; this is the RANS closure problem.

For most two-equation and one-equation models, the Reynolds stresses are approximated with the Boussinesq hypothesis,
\begin{equation}
    -\rho\overline{u_i'u_j'}
    = 2\mu_t S_{ij} - \frac{2}{3}\rho k\delta_{ij},
    \qquad
    S_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial \overline{u_i}}{\partial x_j}
    +\frac{\partial \overline{u_j}}{\partial x_i}\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_t$ is the turbulent (eddy) viscosity and
\begin{equation}
    k=\frac{1}{2}\overline{u_i'u_i'}
\end{equation}
is the turbulent kinetic energy. The practical effect is that turbulence is modelled as an additional momentum diffusion, with $\mu_t$ obtained from one or more transport equations.

RANS is appropriate when the engineering objective is a statistically steady solution, such as mean forces, mean pressure distributions and integrated aerodynamic coefficients. It does not resolve the full turbulent spectrum. Instead, the selected model represents the average influence of turbulence on the resolved flow. Mesh quality, wall treatment, inlet turbulence quantities and convergence therefore affect the accuracy of the result as much as the nominal model choice.

\subsection{Turbulence-model theory for RANS}

\subsubsection{k--epsilon models}

The standard $k$--$\varepsilon$ family solves transport equations for $k$ and the dissipation rate $\varepsilon$. The turbulent viscosity is obtained from
\begin{equation}
    \mu_t = \rho C_\mu \frac{k^2}{\varepsilon}.
\end{equation}
These models are robust and commonly perform well in free-shear flows, mixing layers and fully turbulent regions. They are less reliable in strongly separated flows, adverse pressure gradients and near-wall regions unless an appropriate wall treatment and model variant are used. The repository contains a \codeword{kEpsilon} field template, but it is not currently exposed as a selectable \codeword{TURB_MODEL} in the case-generation mapping.

\subsubsection{k--omega models and SST}

The $k$--$\omega$ family solves for turbulent kinetic energy $k$ and specific dissipation rate $\omega$. A representative eddy-viscosity relation is
\begin{equation}
    \mu_t \sim \rho\frac{k}{\omega}.
\end{equation}
The $\omega$ variable gives the model useful near-wall behaviour, while the formulation can be sensitive to free-stream values. The Shear Stress Transport model, $k$--$\omega$ SST, blends a near-wall $k$--$\omega$ formulation with a more free-stream-robust $k$--$\varepsilon$ behaviour and limits the eddy viscosity in regions of strong strain. This makes SST a common choice for external aerodynamics and separated flows. In \codeword{caseSetup}, the steady alias \codeword{kosst} selects \codeword{kOmegaSST}.

\subsubsection{Spalart--Allmaras}

Spalart--Allmaras is a one-equation model developed primarily for aerodynamic boundary layers. It transports a modified turbulent-viscosity variable, commonly written $\widetilde{\nu}$, and obtains the eddy viscosity through a damping function,
\begin{equation}
    \mu_t = \rho\widetilde{\nu} f_{v1}.
\end{equation}
Its low computational cost and good performance for attached boundary layers make it useful for vehicle aerodynamics. It can be less dependable for complex separation, massive recirculation and strongly three-dimensional vortical flows. The steady alias \codeword{sa} selects \codeword{SpalartAllmaras}.

\subsubsection{GEKO}

GEKO (Generalized k--$\omega$) is a coefficient-tunable two-equation model. It retains the general $k$--$\omega$ structure while allowing calibrated coefficients to alter the response to separation, jets, wakes and free shear. Its results are therefore dependent on both the implementation and the selected coefficient set. The steady alias \codeword{geko} selects \codeword{GEKO}; users should record any GEKO coefficient changes with the case because they directly affect model behaviour.

\subsection{Wall-modelling theory}

The near-wall region contains a viscous sublayer, a buffer layer and an outer logarithmic layer. The dimensionless wall distance is
\begin{equation}
    y^+ = \frac{u_\tau y}{\nu},
    \qquad
    u_\tau = \sqrt{\frac{\tau_w}{\rho}},
\end{equation}
where $y$ is the distance from the wall, $\tau_w$ is wall shear stress and $u_\tau$ is friction velocity. In the viscous sublayer, the mean velocity approximately follows $u^+=y^+$. Farther from the wall, the log-law approximation is commonly written
\begin{equation}
    u^+ = \frac{1}{\kappa}\ln(y^+) + B.
\end{equation}

Resolving the wall means placing the first cell centre inside the viscous sublayer, typically targeting $y^+\approx 1$, and using boundary conditions that integrate the turbulence equations to the wall. This requires many thin, high-aspect-ratio cells and several cells across the boundary layer. Wall functions avoid resolving the viscous sublayer. They impose an algebraic relation between the wall shear stress and the velocity at the first cell centre, reducing the near-wall cell count but requiring the first cell to lie in the range for which the wall function was developed. A mesh with an unsuitable $y^+$ can therefore produce misleading skin friction, separation and aerodynamic forces even when the outer flow appears converged.

The generated cases write turbulence wall boundary conditions from the repository's fluid wall-options template. The choice is made independently for each geometry and ground section with \codeword{WALL_MODEL} or \codeword{GRND_WALL_MODEL}. The available inputs are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \codeword{high}: selects \codeword{highReynolds}. The turbulent viscosity uses \codeword{nutkWallFunction}; $k$, $\varepsilon$ and $\omega$ use their corresponding OpenFOAM wall functions. This is the conventional high-Reynolds-number wall-function treatment and should be paired with a wall-function mesh rather than a nominally wall-resolved mesh.
    \item \codeword{ally}: selects \codeword{allReynolds}. The turbulent viscosity uses \codeword{nutUSpaldingWallFunction}, with wall functions for $k$, $\varepsilon$ and $\omega$. The Spalding formulation provides a blended velocity relation across the near-wall range and is intended to be less dependent on a sharp switch between viscous and logarithmic laws. It does not remove the need to check $y^+$ or guarantee wall-resolved accuracy.
    \item \codeword{low}: selects \codeword{lowReynolds}. The turbulent viscosity uses \codeword{nutLowReWallFunction}; $k$, $\varepsilon$, $\omega$ and the Spalart--Allmaras variable use near-wall fixed values. This is the option intended for a low-$y^+$, wall-resolved treatment. It only behaves as such when the mesh actually resolves the viscous sublayer; selecting \codeword{low} on a coarse wall-function mesh does not create wall resolution.
\end{itemize}

For the Spalart--Allmaras model, the repository sets $\widetilde{\nu}$ to zero at the wall and applies the selected treatment to $\nu_t$. For $k$--$\omega$ SST and GEKO, the $k$ and $\omega$ fields use the corresponding OpenFOAM wall functions in the high- and all-Reynolds options. The $k$--$\varepsilon$ template follows the same high-level wall-function approach, although that model is not currently exposed by the case selector.

There is no separate wall-resolved LES model or automatic wall-model selection in the current setup. The transient IDDES choices inherit wall treatment from their underlying Spalart--Allmaras or SST formulation; they are hybrid RANS--LES models, not wall-resolved LES by default. A \codeword{slip} ground patch has no no-slip wall shear and therefore does not use a turbulence wall function on that patch. Likewise, the \codeword{laminar} template has no turbulent stress model, so the turbulence wall-function discussion does not apply to a truly laminar case.

The solver reports the computed \codeword{yPlus} field through the standard function object. Always inspect its distribution on the vehicle, wheels and ground before interpreting wall shear or force coefficients. A single global average can hide regions of separation, stagnation or excessive cell size, so local maxima and the values over force-producing surfaces are important. Confirm the generated turbulence-properties file before production runs.

\subsection{Other turbulence models in the template library}

The repository also contains templates for hybrid RANS--LES and laminar calculations. These models do not have the same interpretation as a steady RANS mean solution.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{LES}: Large Eddy Simulation resolves the large, energy-containing eddies and models the smaller, more universal scales with a subgrid-scale model. It requires substantially finer spatial and temporal resolution than RANS, particularly near walls.
    \item \textbf{DES}: Detached Eddy Simulation uses a RANS model near attached walls and switches toward LES behaviour in sufficiently resolved separated regions. It is intended to resolve large detached structures without the cost of wall-resolved LES everywhere.
    \item \textbf{DDES}: Delayed Detached Eddy Simulation adds shielding intended to prevent premature switching from RANS to LES inside attached boundary layers. The template library includes \codeword{SpalartAllmarasDDES}.
    \item \textbf{IDDES}: Improved Delayed Detached Eddy Simulation extends the hybrid approach with improved shielding and length-scale treatments. The transient aliases \codeword{sa} and \codeword{kosst} select \codeword{SpalartAllmarasIDDES} and \codeword{kOmegaSSTIDDES}, respectively.
    \item \textbf{Laminar}: A laminar calculation sets the turbulent contribution to zero and solves the molecular-viscosity equations only. The \codeword{laminar} template is present for template development, but it is not currently exposed by the \codeword{TURB_MODEL} selector.
\end{itemize}

The currently exposed choices are therefore \codeword{sa}, \codeword{kosst} and \codeword{geko} for steady cases, and \codeword{sa} and \codeword{kosst} for transient cases. The transient mapping uses hybrid models and should not be interpreted as a time-accurate RANS calculation. Confirm the generated \codeword{constant/turbulencePropertiesSolve} file and the installed ESI OpenFOAM version before production runs, especially when using hybrid models or custom templates.

\subsection{Aero Map Run Selection and Table Building}
\label{sec:rhTheory}

This section explains how \codeword{rideHeightPointGenerator.py} in \codeword{preUtilities} picks the runs and builds the tables. How to use it is covered in Section \codeword{Choosing Ride-Height Points}. You don't need to follow all of the maths to use the script, but it helps to know what it's doing so you can tell when something looks wrong.

The goal is a front ride height (FRH) by rear ride height (RRH) lookup table, usually $6\times6$, that is accurate where the car actually drives, using as few CFD runs as possible. CFD runs are expensive, so we don't want to spend them on ride heights the car never sees. The process has four steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item weight the lap trace, so we know which ride heights matter most
    \item pick the runs
    \item fit a smooth map through the results
    \item place the table breakpoints
\end{itemize}

The figures in this section are made from a real lap trace by \codeword{makeFigures.py}, which is kept with the figures in \codeword{figures/rideHeight}. All the axes that come from the trace are scaled to $0$--$1$, so they show the shape of the data but not the real values. Figures \ref{fig:rhTrend} and \ref{fig:rhTableError} use made-up aero data, not CFD results.

\subsubsection{Weighting the lap trace}

Not every point on the lap is equally important. A ride height matters more if the car carries a lot of load there, spends a long time there, and needs the grip to go faster. To capture this, each sample $i$ in the trace is given a weight,
\begin{equation}
    q_i = V_i^2\,\Delta t_i,
\end{equation}
where $V$ is the speed and $\Delta t$ is the time between samples. Aero load goes up with $V^2$, so $q_i$ is roughly "how much aero load, for how long".

Three weights are then built from $q_i$, each one scaled so it adds up to one over the lap:
\begin{equation}
    w^{g}_i \propto q_i\left(\frac{G_i}{G_{max}}\right)^2, \qquad G_i = \sqrt{a_{x,i}^2 + a_{y,i}^2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    w^{d}_i \propto q_i\,V_i\;\mathbf{1}\!\left[\,|a_{y,i}| < 0.3\,g \;\;\mathrm{and}\;\; \mathrm{throttle}_i > 90\%\,\right],
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    w^{l}_i \propto q_i\left(\frac{|a_{y,i}|}{\max|a_y|}\right)^2 .
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Grip weight} $w^g$ - used for downforce and balance. $G$ is the combined acceleration, so $(G/G_{max})^2$ is close to one when the car is at the limit (corners, braking, traction) and close to zero when it is cruising. Extra downforce only helps lap time when the car is grip limited, so these points count the most.

    \item \textbf{Drag weight} $w^d$ - used for drag. Drag power is $F_D V$, and since $F_D$ goes up with $V^2$, drag power goes up with $V^3$. It only counts on the straights (less than 0.3 g lateral and more than 90\% throttle), because that's where drag costs you top speed. The $\mathbf{1}[\dots]$ is just 1 on a straight and 0 everywhere else. If the lap has no straights, for example a skidpad, the whole lap is used instead and a warning is printed.

    \item \textbf{Lateral weight} $w^l$ - the same idea as the grip weight, but only counting cornering. It is used for the roll, yaw and steer sweeps.
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:rhWeights} shows the difference. The grip weight is spread over the corners, while the drag weight sits almost entirely on the straights. This is why the downforce and drag tables need to be accurate in different places.

\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Weighted breakpoints}

A first guess at the breakpoints comes straight from the trace. The idea is simple: put more breakpoints where the car spends more time.

The front ride heights of all the samples are sorted into 60 bins, and each bin adds up the grip weight of the samples in it. This gives a histogram $h_k$ of where the car spends its (weighted) time. The histogram is smoothed a little, raised to a power $p$, and then added up from left to right:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde h_k = \left(G_\sigma * h\right)_k^{\,p}, \qquad F(x) = \frac{\sum_{x_k \le x}\tilde h_k}{\sum_k \tilde h_k}, \qquad b_j = F^{-1}\!\left(\frac{j}{n-1}\right), \quad j = 0,\dots,n-1.
\end{equation}
$G_\sigma *$ is the smoothing. $F(x)$ goes from 0 at the lowest ride height to 1 at the highest, and the breakpoints $b_j$ are placed where $F$ crosses $0, \frac{1}{n-1}, \frac{2}{n-1}, \dots, 1$. The first and last breakpoints always sit at the lowest and highest ride height in the trace. The same is done for the rear.

The power $p$ controls how much the breakpoints bunch up (Figure \ref{fig:rhBreakpointPower}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item $p = 0$ - the weights are ignored and the breakpoints are evenly spaced
    \item $p = 1$ - every gap holds the same amount of weight. The breakpoints bunch up in the busiest area and leave big gaps everywhere else
    \item $p = 1/3$ - the value used, somewhere in between
\end{itemize}
The value of $1/3$ comes from a known result in signal processing (Panter and Dite): when rounding a signal to a set of levels, the best spacing of the levels follows the cube root of how often each value comes up. A lookup table isn't exactly the same problem, so treat $1/3$ as a sensible starting point, not the perfect answer. The breakpoints are improved later anyway, see Section \ref{sec:rhBreakpointOpt}.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/rideHeight/rhBreakpointPower.png}
    \caption{Top: how much grip weight the car spends at each front ride height. Bottom: the six breakpoints you get with $p = 0$, $1/3$ and $1$. The red ones are what the script uses.}
    \label{fig:rhBreakpointPower}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Choosing the runs}

\textbf{The idea in one line:} we keep track of how sure we are about the aero map everywhere on the lap, and keep adding the run that makes us most sure about the parts that matter.

To do this we use a Gaussian process (GP). You can think of a GP as a smart way to guess the values between runs, and to say how confident that guess is. Close to a run we are confident, far from any run we are not. How quickly the confidence drops off with distance is set by
\begin{equation}
    k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{d \in \{F,R\}} \left(\frac{x_d - x'_d}{\ell_d}\right)^2\right).
\end{equation}
This says that two ride heights close together should give similar results, and two far apart could be anything. The length scales $\ell_F$ and $\ell_R$ set what "close" means. They are set with \codeword{--ls}, default 12 mm front and 15 mm rear. Figure \ref{fig:rhLengthScale} shows the effect in one dimension. With a short length scale the confidence drops off quickly between runs, so you need more runs to cover the same range.

The uncertainty is given as a standard deviation $\sigma$ between 0 and 1. A value of 0 means we know the answer there exactly, and 1 means we know nothing.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rideHeight/rhLengthScale.png}
    \caption{The same four runs with a short and a long length scale $\ell$. The blue line is the best guess and the shaded band is how unsure we are. With a short $\ell$ we quickly become unsure again between runs.}
    \label{fig:rhLengthScale}
\end{figure}

The places a run could go (the candidates) are every second sample of the trace, $\mathbf{c}_1,\dots,\mathbf{c}_N$. Once we have runs at a set of points $S$, the uncertainty between all the candidates is
\begin{equation}
    \Sigma = K_{CC} - K_{CS}\left(K_{SS} + \epsilon I\right)^{-1}K_{SC}.
\end{equation}
Each $K$ is a table of $k$ between two sets of points. The first term is what we knew before any runs, and the second is what the runs have taught us. The small $\epsilon = 10^{-4}$ is only there to keep the maths stable.

Adding a new run at candidate $j$ lowers the uncertainty at every other candidate $i$ by $\Sigma_{ij}^2 / \Sigma_{jj}$. So for each candidate we add up how much uncertainty it would remove, weighted by how important each point is, and pick the best one:
\begin{equation}
    j^{*} = \mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{j}\; \frac{\sum_i m_i\,\Sigma_{ij}^2}{\Sigma_{jj}}, \qquad m_i = \alpha\,\bar w^{g}_i + \frac{1 - \alpha}{N}, \quad \alpha = 0.6.
\end{equation}
$m_i$ is how important candidate $i$ is. It is 60\% grip weight and 40\% spread evenly over all the candidates. The even part makes sure ride heights the car rarely visits still get some coverage, because they still end up in the table.

A few rules on top of this:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The first two runs are always the reference ride height and the lowest heave point (lowest $dFRH + dRRH$) in the trace.
    \item No run can be within 8 mm of another. If every candidate is within 8 mm of a run, the script stops early and prints a warning.
    \item All runs are rounded to 0.5 mm.
\end{itemize}

One important point: the uncertainty only depends on \textit{where} the runs are, not on their results. This is why the whole run list can be made before any CFD is run. The uncertainty at any single point is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{1 - \mathbf{k}_S(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\left(K_{SS} + \epsilon I\right)^{-1}\mathbf{k}_S(\mathbf{x})}.
\end{equation}
Figure \ref{fig:rhSelectionSteps} shows it shrinking as runs are added, and Figure \ref{fig:rhLengthScaleLayout} shows how the run layout changes with the length scale.

    \label{fig:rhSelectionSteps}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Validation runs} are held out of the fit and used to check it. They are most useful where the car spends a lot of time but the fit runs are furthest away, so each candidate is given a score
\begin{equation}
    s_j = \rho_j\,\sigma(\mathbf{c}_j), \qquad \rho_j = \sum_{i:\,|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{c}_j| < 3\,\mathrm{mm}} w_i .
\end{equation}
$\rho_j$ adds up the weight of the trace samples within 3 mm of the candidate, so it is high where the car spends time. $\sigma$ is high where the fit runs are far away. The first validation run uses the drag weight and the second uses the grip weight. A validation run must be more than 4 mm from every fit run and more than 8 mm from the other validation runs.

\subsubsection{Roll, yaw and steer sweeps}

We assume the car is the same on the left and right. That means 2 degrees of roll in a left hander gives the mirror image of 2 degrees in a right hander, so only positive angles need to be run.

The breakpoints for each angle are found the same way as the ride-height breakpoints, using the lateral weight on the size of the angle. A few extra details:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A breakpoint at 0 is always included.
    \item The top 1\% of the weight is ignored when spacing the breakpoints, so one extreme sample doesn't stretch everything out. The last breakpoint is still the biggest angle in the trace, rounded up.
    \item Breakpoints are rounded to 0.1, 0.5 or 1 degree, depending on how big the biggest angle is.
\end{itemize}

The sweeps are run at the cornering ride height $\mathbf{x}_{rh1}$, which is the weighted average ride height in corners:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{rh1} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}} w^l_i\,\mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{C}} w^l_i}, \qquad \mathcal{C} = \{\,i : |a_{y,i}| > 1\,g\,\}.
\end{equation}
$\mathcal{C}$ is every sample with more than 1 g lateral. Because it is an average, it can land between the lines the car actually drives, you can see this in Figure \ref{fig:rhSweeps}. The second ride height $\mathbf{x}_{rh2}$ is the busiest cornering ride height that is at least 8 mm away from $\mathbf{x}_{rh1}$.

The sweeps rely on two assumptions, and each one has runs to check it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The effect of each angle doesn't change with ride height.} To check this, the middle and top level of each sweep are run again at $\mathbf{x}_{rh2}$. If the change in force $\Delta C$ is different there, the angle effects need their own ride-height dependence.

    \item \textbf{The effects add up.}
    \begin{equation}
        C(\mathbf{x}, \phi, \beta, \delta) \approx C(\mathbf{x}, 0, 0, 0) + \Delta C_\phi(\phi) + \Delta C_\beta(\beta) + \Delta C_\delta(\delta).
    \end{equation}
    In words, the force with roll $\phi$, yaw $\beta$ and steer $\delta$ all together should be the force with none of them, plus the change from each one on its own. To check this, two combined runs are done, one for a moderate corner and one for a hard corner (the hardest quarter of the corners). Each uses the typical (median) roll, yaw and steer for that kind of corner.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Fitting the results}

Once the runs are solved, we need a smooth map through the results so we can read off a value anywhere, including at the table breakpoints. Each quantity (for example $C_{zF}$) is fitted in two parts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a quadratic, a simple curved surface that captures the overall trend
    \item a GP on top, to pick up the details the quadratic misses
\end{itemize}
The ride heights are first scaled so each axis has an average of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, $\mathbf{z} = (\mathbf{x} - \mu)/\mathbf{s}$. Then
\begin{equation}
    \hat y(\mathbf{z}) = \phi(\mathbf{z})^{\top}\beta + s_r\,g(\mathbf{z}), \qquad \phi(\mathbf{z}) = \left[\,1,\; z_F,\; z_R,\; z_F^2,\; z_F z_R,\; z_R^2\,\right]^{\top}.
\end{equation}
The first part is the quadratic, its coefficients $\beta$ are a least-squares fit to the runs. Whatever the quadratic misses, $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{y} - \Phi\beta$, is scaled by its standard deviation $s_r$ and fitted with the GP $g$. This GP uses the same kind of covariance as before, plus a noise term:
\begin{equation}
    k_g(\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{z}') = c\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_d \left(\frac{z_d - z'_d}{\lambda_d}\right)^2\right) + \sigma_n^2\,\delta_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}'} .
\end{equation}
Unlike the GP used to pick the runs, this one learns its settings from the results. The size $c$, the length scales $\lambda_F$ and $\lambda_R$, and the noise $\sigma_n^2$ are chosen to make the results as likely as possible:
\begin{equation}
    \log p(\mathbf{r} \mid \theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{r}^{\top}K_g^{-1}\mathbf{r} - \frac{1}{2}\log|K_g| - \frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi.
\end{equation}
You don't need to follow this equation. It balances fitting the runs closely against keeping the map smooth. It is tried from five random starting points so it doesn't get stuck on a bad answer. The noise term lets the map pass close to, but not exactly through, runs that are a little noisy from convergence.

Why bother with the quadratic? It matters at the edges of the table. Far from any run, a GP on its own just drifts back to the average of the data, while the quadratic keeps following the overall curve (Figure \ref{fig:rhTrend}). The quadratic has six coefficients, so it needs a lot more than six runs, which is why the default is 16.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/rideHeight/rhSurrogateTrend.png}
    \caption{A made-up example in one dimension. Inside the grey area, where the runs are, both fits agree. Outside it, the GP on its own flattens out towards the average, while the quadratic plus GP keeps the curve going. Neither should be trusted far from the runs, and the quadratic only helps if the real aero curve is roughly quadratic.}
    \label{fig:rhTrend}
\end{figure}

To check the fit, each run is left out in turn, the map is refitted without it, and the error at that run is measured, $e_i = \hat y_{-i}(\mathbf{x}_i) - y_i$. This is called the leave-one-out error. It is printed along with the error at each validation run, both as a percentage of the range of $y$.

\subsubsection{Table error and breakpoint optimisation}
\label{sec:rhBreakpointOpt}

The table stores the fitted map at the breakpoints, and values in between are found by bilinear interpolation, which basically draws straight lines between the breakpoints. The table is exact at the breakpoints, so all of the error comes from those straight lines. Across a gap of width $h$, the error from a straight line is at most
\begin{equation}
    |e| \le \frac{h^2}{8}\,\max|f''|,
\end{equation}
where $f''$ is how curved the map is. So you want breakpoints close together where the map is very curved and the car spends time, and they can be further apart everywhere else.

Rather than guess, the script measures this directly. For each quantity $k$ it works out the weighted RMS difference between the table $T_k$ and the fitted map $\hat y_k$ over the trace:
\begin{equation}
    E_k = \sqrt{\sum_i \bar w_{k,i}\left(T_k(\mathbf{x}_i) - \hat y_k(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
Drag quantities use the drag weight and everything else uses the grip weight. To combine the quantities, each one is divided by its range $R_k$, so a quantity with big numbers doesn't drown out the others:
\begin{equation}
    J = \sqrt{\sum_k \left(\frac{E_k}{R_k}\right)^2}.
\end{equation}
The script then moves the inner breakpoints around to make $J$ as small as possible, using the Nelder--Mead method and starting from the weighted breakpoints. The first and last breakpoints don't move. To make sure the breakpoints never cross over each other, the optimiser actually moves the gaps between them, $g_m = e^{p_m}$, which are always positive:
\begin{equation}
    b_j = b_0 + \left(b_{n-1} - b_0\right)\frac{\sum_{m<j} g_m}{\sum_m g_m}.
\end{equation}
After that the breakpoints are rounded to 0.5 mm. If rounding puts two breakpoints on top of each other, the weighted breakpoints are kept instead. Figure \ref{fig:rhTableError} shows the result for a made-up rear downforce map.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rideHeight/rhTableError.png}
    \caption{The error between the table and the fitted map for a made-up $C_{zR}$, with the weighted breakpoints (left) and the optimised breakpoints (right). The dotted lines are the breakpoints and the grey dots are the lap trace. The number in each title is the weighted RMS error as a percentage of the range. Moving the rear breakpoints spreads the error out more evenly and lowers it overall.}
    \label{fig:rhTableError}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The table is only as good as the fitted map, and the fitted map is only as good as the runs. The table error $J$ only compares the table to the fitted map, not to CFD. The only checks against real CFD are the leave-one-out and validation errors, so always look at them.

    \item The runs are picked assuming the aero changes smoothly with ride height. If something sudden happens, like the floor stalling, it can be missed between two runs. If you expect this, lower \codeword{--ls} or add runs in that area.

    \item The weights come from one lap with one setup. A different track or setup changes which ride heights matter, so the runs should be picked again.

    \item The sweep assumptions are only checked at two ride heights and two combined corners.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Suspension Kinematics}
\label{sec:suspTheory}

This section explains what happens to each ride-height point when the kinematic solver is turned on with \codeword{USE_KINEMATIC_SOLVER}. The solver code is in the \codeword{rideHeightUtils} folder. How to set it up is covered in Section \codeword{Ride-Height and Suspension Kinematics}. Again, you don't need to follow every equation to use it, but knowing what it does makes it much easier to spot a bad hardpoint file.

There are three steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item turn the four corner ride heights into a pitch, roll and heave of the car, and a wheel travel at each corner
    \item for each corner, solve the linkage to find where every part sits at that wheel travel (and steer)
    \item move each part of the geometry to its new position
\end{itemize}

The figures in this section are made by the \codeword{makeFigures.py} script in \codeword{figures/suspension}. It runs the real solver on the hardpoint template. The drawings have no scale, and the plots show every value as a fraction of its largest value, so they show the shape of the results but not the real numbers.

\subsubsection{From corner ride heights to pitch, roll and heave}

The ride-height file gives a change in height $\Delta z$ at each corner, positive when that corner of the car goes up. These are first turned into the motion of the whole car. The front and rear are averaged across the car, and the left and right are averaged along it:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta z_F = \frac{\Delta z_{FL} + \Delta z_{FR}}{2}, \qquad \Delta z_R = \frac{\Delta z_{RL} + \Delta z_{RR}}{2}, \qquad h = \frac{\Delta z_F + \Delta z_R}{2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \theta = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\Delta z_F - \Delta z_R}{L}\right), \qquad \phi = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{\Delta z_{FR} - \Delta z_{FL}}{t}\right).
\end{equation}
$h$ is the heave, how far the middle of the car moves up. $\theta$ is the pitch, positive when the front goes up. $\phi$ is the roll, positive when the right side goes up. $L$ is the wheelbase and $t$ the track width, taken from \codeword{RH_REF_LEN} and \codeword{RH_REF_WIDTH}, or from \codeword{REFLEN} and \codeword{REFWIDTH} when those are blank. Figure \ref{fig:suspPitchRoll} shows what each one means.

The roll is worked out twice, once from the front pair of corners and once from the rear pair. A rigid car can only heave, pitch and roll, which is three numbers, but the file gives four. So the four corners have to sit on a flat plane, otherwise the chassis would have to twist. If the front and rear roll differ by more than $0.05^\circ$, \codeword{caseSetup} stops with an error, because the point is asking for something the car can't do.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/suspension/suspPitchRoll.png}
    \caption{How the corner changes become pitch (left) and roll (right). The dashed box is the car at its original height. Heave is the average height change, measured at the middle of the car.}
    \label{fig:suspPitchRoll}
\end{figure}

In CFD it is easier to keep the car still and move everything around it. So instead of moving the car body, \codeword{caseSetup} moves the ground and tunnel by the opposite amount: down by $h$, and rotated by $-\theta$ and $-\phi$ about \codeword{REFCOR}. The car body looks exactly the same to the flow either way.

The wheels are the opposite story. If the car goes up at a corner and the tyre stays on the ground, then seen from the car the wheel has dropped. So the wheel travel at each corner, measured from the car body, is
\begin{equation}
    s = -\Delta z,
\end{equation}
positive in bump (wheel moving up towards the body) and negative in droop. With the simple mode the wheel is just moved up or down by $s$. With the kinematic solver $s$ is the input to the linkage solve below.

\subsubsection{The corner as a linkage}

Figure \ref{fig:suspCornerLayout} shows the front-left corner from the hardpoint template. The upright, which carries the wheel, is treated as one solid part. It is held in place by five rods, each with a fixed length:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the upper control arm (UCA), which is two rods from the chassis meeting at one ball joint on the upright
    \item the lower control arm (LCA), also two rods and one ball joint
    \item the tie rod, from the steering rack to the upright
\end{itemize}
On top of that, the pushrod (or pullrod) runs from one of the wishbones to the rocker, the rocker turns about its pivot, and the damper runs from the rocker to the chassis.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/suspension/suspCornerLayout.png}
    \caption{The front-left corner of the hardpoint template, seen from behind. Black squares are fixed to the chassis, white circles move with the wheel. The two legs of each wishbone overlap from this angle. The rocker is the shaded triangle.}
    \label{fig:suspCornerLayout}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The idea in one line:} if you know how long every rod is, and how high the wheel is, there is only one way the corner can sit.

To find that position the solver needs eight numbers, put together in a list $\mathbf{x}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item three for how the upright is rotated, written as a rotation vector $\mathbf{r}$ (its direction is the axis, its length is the angle)
    \item three for where the wheel centre $\mathbf{c}$ is
    \item the rocker angle $\beta$
    \item how far the steering rack has moved, $d$
\end{itemize}
Every point bolted to the upright then moves with it. If $\mathbf{p}^0$ is a point on the upright measured from the static wheel centre, its new position is $R(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{p}^0 + \mathbf{c}$, where $R(\mathbf{r})$ is the rotation matrix for $\mathbf{r}$.

Eight unknowns need eight equations. Each one is written as an error $r_k$ that should be zero:
\begin{equation}
    r_k = \left\| R(\mathbf{r})\,\mathbf{p}^0_k + \mathbf{c} - \mathbf{q}_k \right\| - L_k, \qquad k = 1\dots5,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    r_6 = \left\| \mathbf{p}_{push} - \mathbf{k}_{push}(\beta) \right\| - L_{push}, \qquad r_7 = c_z - (c_z^0 + s), \qquad r_8 = \mathrm{steer\ equation}.
\end{equation}
In words:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_1$ to $r_5$ - each of the five rods must keep its static length $L_k$. $\mathbf{q}_k$ is the chassis end of the rod and $\mathbf{p}^0_k$ the upright end. For the tie rod the chassis end is the rack, which slides by $d$ along \codeword{RACK_AXIS}.
    \item $r_6$ - the pushrod must keep its length. Its lower end $\mathbf{p}_{push}$ is bolted to a wishbone, so it swings about that wishbone's two chassis mounts by the same angle as the wishbone. Its upper end $\mathbf{k}_{push}$ is on the rocker, turned by $\beta$ about the rocker axis.
    \item $r_7$ - the wheel centre must be at the requested height, the static height plus the travel $s$.
    \item $r_8$ - with no steer, the rack stays put ($d = 0$). When steering the front-left, the toe angle must equal the requested angle. For the front-right, the rack travel must equal the front-left rack travel (see below).
\end{itemize}
Everything is in metres, so the steer error is multiplied by the tie rod length to turn an angle into a length. That way all eight errors are the same kind of number and can be added up fairly.

\subsubsection{How the solver finds the answer}

The equations can't be solved directly, so the solver guesses and corrects (Gauss--Newton). Starting from a guess $\mathbf{x}$, it checks how much each error $r_k$ changes when each unknown is nudged a little. This table of slopes is called the Jacobian $J$. It is then used to work out the step that should bring all the errors to zero together:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mathbf{x} = -\left(J^{T}J + \lambda I\right)^{-1} J^{T}\,\mathbf{r}.
\end{equation}
You don't need to follow this equation. It is the "least squares" step: the change in $\mathbf{x}$ that best cancels the current errors if everything were a straight line. The tiny $\lambda$ ($10^{-9}$) keeps it from blowing up when the linkage is close to locking.

The equations aren't straight lines, so a full step can overshoot. If the step makes the total error bigger, it is halved and tried again, up to ten times. The rocker angle is also kept within $\pm 90^\circ$. The loop stops when the total error is below $10^{-9}$ m, or after 80 steps.

Figure \ref{fig:suspSolverConvergence} shows how fast this is. Even starting from the static pose, the error drops by roughly a hundred times each step and passes in two to three steps. In \codeword{caseSetup} each point starts from the answer of the point before it, which makes it even quicker.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/suspension/suspSolverConvergence.png}
    \caption{The total error after each solver step for three cases on the template corner. Note the log scale. Anything under the dashed line passes.}
    \label{fig:suspSolverConvergence}
\end{figure}

A point passes when the total error is under $10^{-6}$ m (0.001 mm). If it doesn't, the linkage really can't reach that position, and \codeword{caseSetup} stops with the \codeword{suspension solve did not converge} error. That error lists which of the eight equations are furthest off, which usually points straight at the problem: travel past what the linkage can do, a typo in a hardpoint, or the wrong \codeword{HARDPOINT_SCALE}.

\subsubsection{What comes out}

From the solved upright, the solver works out the wheel's spin axis $\mathbf{a}$ and forward direction $\mathbf{f}$, by rotating \codeword{WHEEL_AXIS_LOCAL} and \codeword{WHEEL_FORWARD_LOCAL}. From these:
\begin{equation}
    \gamma = \sin^{-1}\!\left(a_z\right), \qquad \delta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{f_y}{f_x}\right), \qquad \Delta l_{damper} = \left\| \mathbf{k}_{damper}(\beta) - \mathbf{q}_{damper} \right\| - l^0_{damper}.
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\gamma$ is the camber. The spin axis $\mathbf{a}$ is flipped first, if needed, so it always points inboard. That way the sign doesn't depend on which way \codeword{WHEEL_AXIS_LOCAL} was typed in. Negative camber means the top of the wheel leans in towards the car. Only the vertical part of the axis is used, so steering doesn't change it.
    \item $\delta$ is the toe (or steer) angle, the direction the wheel points in the ground plane.
    \item $\Delta l_{damper}$ is how much the damper has stretched, negative when compressed. $\mathbf{k}_{damper}$ is the rocker end of the damper, $\mathbf{q}_{damper}$ the chassis end.
\end{itemize}
All of these are written into the ride-height table for every point and corner, together with the rocker angle and rack travel.

Figure \ref{fig:suspCurves} shows them over the travel range for the template's front-left corner. How much the damper moves for each millimetre of wheel travel is the motion ratio,
\begin{equation}
    MR = -\frac{d\,\Delta l_{damper}}{ds},
\end{equation}
which is just the slope of the right-hand plot. A straight line means the motion ratio stays about the same through the travel.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/suspension/suspCurves.png}
    \caption{Camber change, toe change (bump steer) and damper length change against wheel travel, for the front-left of the hardpoint template. Every axis is scaled to the largest value in that plot.}
    \label{fig:suspCurves}
\end{figure}

These curves are a good check of your hardpoint file. If the camber or bump steer looks very different to what your suspension design says, a hardpoint is probably wrong.

\subsubsection{Steering}

Both front wheels are connected to one steering rack. So the front-left and front-right can't be given angles separately, they have to share one rack travel. \codeword{caseSetup} does this in two passes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the front-left is solved with $r_8$ set to "toe must equal the requested steer angle". This gives the rack travel $d$ needed.
    \item the front-right is then solved with $r_8$ set to "rack travel must equal $d$". Its angle comes out of the geometry.
\end{itemize}
Because of the way the steering arms are angled, the same rack travel gives a different angle at each wheel. This difference is Ackermann, and Figure \ref{fig:suspAckermann} shows it for the template. It grows with steer angle and depends on which way you are turning. You don't set it anywhere, it simply comes out of the hardpoints, the same as it would on the real car. The front-right also sits at its own ride height, so the difference includes any bump steer too.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/suspension/suspAckermann.png}
    \caption{Left: the front-right angle for the same rack travel as the front-left, against the dashed line where both wheels would steer the same. Right: the difference between the two. Both at static ride height, with both axes scaled by the largest angle.}
    \label{fig:suspAckermann}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Moving the geometry}

Once a corner is solved, every part is moved as one rigid piece using
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{v}' = R(\mathbf{r}_c)\left(\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{o}_c\right) + \mathbf{o}_c + \mathbf{t}_c,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}$ is each vertex of the part, and each component $c$ has its own rotation $\mathbf{r}_c$, pivot point $\mathbf{o}_c$ and shift $\mathbf{t}_c$. In words: rotate the part about its pivot, then shift it. The rotation matrix comes from Rodrigues' formula,
\begin{equation}
    R = I + \sin\alpha\,K + (1 - \cos\alpha)\,K^2,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the length of $\mathbf{r}_c$ (the angle) and $K$ is a matrix built from its direction (the axis). You never need to work this out by hand, it is just the standard way to turn "rotate by this angle about this axis" into numbers.

How the rotation, pivot and shift are chosen for each component is listed in Section \codeword{Per-Component Transforms}. The main ideas are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the wheel and upright take the full solved pose of the upright
    \item the wishbones only rotate about the line through their two chassis mounts, so the mounts stay exactly where they are
    \item the rocker only rotates about its own axis
    \item the pushrod and tie rod are rotated and shifted so both of their ends land on the parts they are attached to
    \item the damper turns about its chassis end and is stretched along its length by the damper stroke, so it gets shorter in bump
\end{itemize}
Figure \ref{fig:suspPoses} shows the result at full droop, static and full bump. For every child case, \codeword{caseSetup} also saves a 3D before and after plot of each corner, \codeword{linkage_validation_<corner>.png}, in the child folder. It prints a warning if any part turns or moves much more than a suspension normally would. Have a quick look at these the first time you use a new hardpoint file.

    \caption{The template front-left corner at full droop (green), static (black) and full bump (red), seen from behind. The chassis mounts don't move, the wheel gains negative camber in bump, and the rocker turns to compress the damper.}

\subsubsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Every part is treated as perfectly stiff. Bushes, flex in the arms and compliance steer are not modelled.

    \item The tyre isn't squashed. The ride height is the body height, so all of the body motion goes into wheel travel. In reality some of it is tyre deflection.

    \item Camber and toe are measured relative to the car body, not the ground. When the car rolls, the angle to the ground is different by about the roll angle.

    \item The four corners have to sit on a flat plane, a ride-height point can't twist the chassis.

    \item Each corner is solved on its own. Anything joining the two sides, like an anti-roll bar, isn't modelled, and its parts won't move unless they are attached to a corner in the hardpoint file.

    \item Only the front is steered, the rack moves in one straight line, and the front-left is always the reference wheel.

    \item The solver finds the position closest to where it started. For normal travel there is only one sensible answer, but for a linkage near its limit, check the linkage plots.
\end{itemize}
